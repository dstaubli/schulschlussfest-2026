Swiss Federal Tax Administration

Transfer Pricing

Principles, methods, documentation requirements, and procedural aspects applicable to transactions between associated enterprises under Swiss law.

Legislation as of1 January 2024
PublisherSwiss Tax Conference (SSK / CSI)
EditorFederal Tax Administration (FTA)
Section 1

Introduction

Transfer pricing refers to the prices and other conditions under which an enterprise transfers physical goods, intangible assets or services to an associated enterprise — in essence, an enterprise from the same group. Given the large number of multinational enterprise (MNE) groups operating in the global economy today, this is a very important issue in international tax law.

Switzerland, like many other countries, applies the principle of separate entity taxation (separation principle). Under this approach, each company with legal personality that forms part of a multinational group is treated as a taxpayer in its own right and taxed individually on the profits it makes.

Under the separation principle, transfer pricing applied between associated enterprises must comply with the arm's length principle. This principle requires transactions between entities of a multinational group to meet the same conditions as those which would be agreed between independent enterprises. Otherwise, the profits which would have accrued to one of the entities may be adjusted and taxed accordingly.

Example

A company in Country A pays royalties to a parent company owning a brand in Country B. Because royalties are a deductible expense for Company A and taxable income for the parent in Country B, their determination directly affects how group profits are allocated between the two countries. Under the arm's length principle, the royalties must be set at a price two independent enterprises would agree upon.

The separation principle and the arm's length principle stand in contrast to the unitary approach and global formulary apportionment, under which a multinational group's consolidated profit would be allocated among its constituent entities via a predetermined formula based on factors such as costs, assets, payroll and sales. This approach has not yet gained currency between jurisdictions.

This publication focuses exclusively on transfer pricing in an international context — namely on prices applicable to transactions between companies from the same group based in different countries.

Section 2

Arm's Length Principle

2.1 General Remarks

The arm's length principle is used to determine transfer prices between associated enterprises for tax purposes. It requires that transactions between associated enterprises, regardless of their type, meet the same conditions as those which would be agreed between non-associated third parties under comparable circumstances.

2.2 Legal Basis

2.2.1 International Tax Law

In international tax law, the normative basis for the arm's length principle is found in double taxation agreements (DTAs) signed between states, and specifically in provisions based on Art. 9 of the OECD Model.

The arm's length principle is explained in the OECD Guidelines (most recent version: 2022). Although its contents are not legally binding, Swiss tax authorities and courts increasingly refer to and apply it as a means of interpreting the arm's length principle under Swiss law.

2.2.2 Swiss Tax Law

Swiss tax law does not include specific legislation on transfer pricing. The arm's length principle is implemented through:

Profit tax: Art. 58 para. 1 DFTA and Art. 24 para. 1 DTHA

Withholding tax: Art. 4 para. 1 let. b WTA

Administrative guidelines include FTA Circular No. 4 (19 March 2004, "Taxation of service companies") and FTA Circular Letter No. 203 (7 February 2023) setting safe harbour interest rates for financial transactions.

Non-compliance with the safe harbour guidelines creates a rebuttable presumption of non-compliance with the arm's length principle. Taxpayers may demonstrate that their transaction in fact complies.

2.3 Principal Implications of Non-Compliance

2.3.1 Profit Tax

If a Swiss company pays excessive remuneration to shareholders or close associates abroad (or receives inadequate remuneration from them), the inappropriate portion is considered a hidden profit distribution and is reintegrated into the company's taxable income.

Where a Swiss parent company pays excessive remuneration to a foreign subsidiary (or receives inadequate remuneration from it), this constitutes a hidden capital contribution — equivalent to commercially unjustified depreciation — and may likewise be reintegrated into taxable profit.

2.3.2 Withholding Tax

The excessive portion of remuneration paid to shareholders or close associates abroad is considered a significant monetary benefit subject to withholding tax under Art. 4 para. 1 let. b of the WTA.

Section 3

Comparability Analysis

3.1 General Remarks

The comparability analysis is central to the application of the arm's length principle. It involves reviewing the controlled transaction (the transaction between associated enterprises for which an arm's length price is sought) alongside potentially comparable uncontrolled transactions (transactions between independent enterprises).

The following comparability factors must be identified when accurately delimiting the controlled transaction:

• The contractual terms of the controlled transaction

• Functions performed by each party, taking into account assets used and risks assumed (functional analysis)

• Characteristics of property transferred or services provided

• Economic circumstances of the parties and the market

• Business strategies pursued by the parties

3.2 The Nine Steps of the Comparability Analysis

1
Determination of years to be included

The analysis should ideally be based on data for the year under review and prior years. Analyses based on data too far removed in time will generally not be accepted by Swiss tax authorities.

2
Broad-based analysis of the taxpayer's circumstances

Reviews the taxpayer's economic environment: geographic location, competition, supply and demand, purchasing power, regulatory factors, production and transport costs, and market level (retail or wholesale).

3
Review of the controlled transaction and choice of tested party

Essential for choosing the tested party, selecting the most appropriate TP method, and identifying comparables. The tested party is typically the one with the least complex functional profile and for which the most reliable comparables can be found.

4
Internal comparables

Comparable transactions between one party to the controlled transaction and an independent party (e.g. a taxpayer selling the same product to both an associated enterprise and an independent enterprise).

5
External comparables

Comparable transactions between two independent enterprises neither of which is a party to the controlled transaction. Sources include commercial databases such as Bloomberg, Loan Connector, and TP Catalyst. The FTA does not use non-public "secret comparables."

6
Selection of the most appropriate transfer pricing method

Chosen in accordance with the principles set out in Section 4.

7
Identification of potential comparables

Two approaches may be combined: the additive approach (spontaneously listing potentially comparable independent enterprises) and the deductive approach (starting from a broad database search of companies in the same sector).

8
Comparability adjustments

Where significant differences exist between the controlled and comparable transactions, adjustments may be made to eliminate the effects of those differences. If adjustments required are too numerous or substantial, the comparable may not be sufficiently reliable.

9
Determination of the arm's length remuneration

Applying a TP method generally yields a range of comparable remunerations (arm's length range). In practice, the interquartile range (values between the 25th and 75th percentile) is the most frequently used tool. All values within this range are generally in accordance with the arm's length principle.

Section 4

Transfer Pricing Methods

4.1 Traditional Transaction Methods

CUP Method

Comparable Uncontrolled Price

Compares the price charged in a controlled transaction with the price in a comparable transaction between independent enterprises. Most appropriate where internal comparables exist, for commodities at quoted prices (oil, metals, agricultural products), or for interest rates.

RPM

Resale Price Method

Starts with the resale price to an independent enterprise and subtracts an appropriate gross margin. Primarily used for distribution companies. Functional analysis is more crucial than product similarity under this method.

CPM

Cost Plus Method

Determines costs incurred in a controlled transaction and adds an appropriate cost plus mark-up. Suitable for production companies with certain functional profiles (e.g. manufacturers of semi-finished goods) and service providers.

4.2 Transactional Profit Methods

Unlike traditional methods that focus on prices, transactional profit methods examine the profits arising from a transaction.

TNMM

Transactional Net Margin Method

Determines the net profit realised from a controlled transaction by reference to a net profit indicator earned in comparable uncontrolled transactions. Net profit indicators include return on assets and operating income to sales. Very frequently used in practice due to the wide availability of public data.

PSM

Profit Split Method

Identifies profits made under a controlled transaction and splits them between associated enterprises based on their respective contributions, approximating the division independent enterprises would have agreed upon. Appropriate where each party makes unique and valuable contributions, or where business operations are highly integrated.

4.3 Other Methods

The methods recognised by the OECD Guidelines should be given priority. Other methods may be used only when justified by the facts and circumstances of the individual case, and only if the resulting prices comply with the arm's length principle.

Section 5

Special Areas of Application

The OECD Guidelines (Chapters VI–X) include special considerations for certain specific transactions, in addition to the general principles above.

5.2 Intangible Assets

Intangible assets (patents, know-how, trade secrets, trademarks) are items that are not tangible or financial assets, are capable of being owned or controlled for use in commercial activities, and whose use or transfer would be remunerated in a transaction between independent parties.

The functional analysis for intangible assets involves the DEMPE functions:

D – Development  |  E – Enhancement  |  M – Maintenance  |  P – Protection  |  E – Exploitation

Income related to intangible assets does not necessarily have to be assigned to their legal owner. Each group member must receive appropriate remuneration for the DEMPE functions they perform.

Where comparable uncontrolled data is unavailable, valuation techniques — particularly income-based techniques premised on discounted projected future cash flows — may be used to estimate arm's length prices.

5.3 Intragroup Services

Intragroup services encompass a broad range of administrative, technical, financial and commercial activities. For low value-adding intragroup services, the OECD has developed a simplified approach:

Simplified Approach – Low Value-Adding Services

Low value-adding services are supportive in nature, not part of the group's core business, do not require unique intangible assets, and do not involve significant risk. Examples include accounting, human resources, and legal and tax services.

A flat 5% profit margin may be applied to all such services without requiring a comparative analysis.

5.4 Financial Transactions

Chapter X of the 2022 OECD Guidelines (incorporating guidance adopted in 2020) covers transfer pricing aspects of financial transactions, including intragroup loans, cash pooling, hedging, financial guarantees, and captive insurance companies.

Key principles include the use of credit ratings (per major agencies: Moody's, Standard & Poor's, Fitch) to assess borrower creditworthiness and identify comparables for interest rate determination. Additionally, any implicit support — the benefit a company receives from group membership in meeting its financial obligations — must be taken into account when assessing creditworthiness, though this benefit itself should not be subject to remuneration.

Section 6

Documentation

The OECD Guidelines envisage a three-tiered documentation structure:

MF
Master File

Provides an overview of the group's business and its transfer pricing policy, for use across all jurisdictions.

LF
Local File

Provides detailed information on the intragroup transactions to which each group entity is a party.

CbCR
Country-by-Country Report

Provides aggregate information on the global allocation of the group's income and taxes paid in each country.

Swiss Requirement

In Switzerland, the only mandatory transfer pricing documentation is the Country-by-Country Report. Groups whose parent company is taxable in Switzerland and which generate turnover exceeding CHF 900 million must prepare a CbCR (Art. 6 CbCRAA), which is automatically exchanged with relevant tax authorities.

Master and local files are not specifically required by Swiss law. However, under the general cooperation obligation (Art. 126 DFTA; Art. 39 WTA), taxpayers must provide documents demonstrating compliance with the arm's length principle upon request.

Section 7

Procedural Aspects

7.1 Competent Authorities

Cantonal tax authorities – Responsible for profit tax, including rulings on profit tax and primary/corresponding adjustments.

Federal Tax Administration (FTA) – Responsible for withholding tax, rulings on withholding tax, and secondary adjustments.

State Secretariat for International Finance (SIF) – Solely responsible for negotiating advance pricing agreements (APAs) and mutual agreements with partner states.

7.2 Rulings and Advance Pricing Agreements

7.2.1 Rulings

A ruling is binding information from a tax authority on the tax treatment of a planned, specific transaction. Swiss authorities review ruling applications on all aspects of taxation, including transfer pricing — confirming that proposed intercompany terms comply with the arm's length principle and have no impact on profit or withholding tax.

Given that the same transaction may affect both taxes, it is recommended to file simultaneously with the competent cantonal authority and with the FTA. Applications must include appropriate supporting documentation (e.g. a transfer pricing study).

7.2.2 Bilateral or Multilateral Advance Pricing Agreements

Unlike unilateral rulings, APAs are jointly negotiated and granted by the competent authorities of several states, securing the tax position of associated enterprises in all countries affected. In Switzerland, only the SIF negotiates APAs with partner states.

7.3 Primary and Corresponding Adjustments

7.3.1 Primary Adjustment

A primary adjustment is an adjustment of a company's taxable profits made by a tax authority as a result of applying the arm's length principle. In Switzerland, primary adjustments are made exclusively by cantonal tax authorities.

Example

Company A in Switzerland pays CHF 50,000 in royalties to its parent Company B abroad. The cantonal authority determines that the arm's length price would be CHF 30,000 and reintegrates CHF 20,000 into Company A's taxable profit. This is a primary adjustment.

7.3.2 Corresponding Adjustment and Mutual Agreement Procedure

A corresponding adjustment reduces the taxable profit of the associated enterprise in the other state by the amount of the primary adjustment, eliminating the economic double taxation created by the primary adjustment.

In practice, corresponding adjustments are made through the mutual agreement procedure (MAP), provided for by Art. 9 para. 1 of the OECD Model, regulated in Swiss domestic law since 1 January 2022 by Art. 2–23 of the ITAIA. A mutual agreement accepted by the taxpayer is binding on the tax authorities of all states involved.

7.4 Secondary Adjustment

A secondary adjustment imposes tax on the significant monetary benefit paid to an associated enterprise as a result of a transfer price that is excessive under the arm's length principle. In Switzerland, secondary adjustments are made exclusively by the FTA through withholding tax, independently of whether a primary adjustment has been made by the cantonal authority.

Example (continued)

Following the primary adjustment above, the FTA would levy withholding tax on CHF 20,000 as a significant monetary benefit paid by Company A to Company B. This constitutes the secondary adjustment.

Where a mutual agreement procedure has been initiated following a primary adjustment, SIF and the FTA may agree that the secondary adjustment will only be levied in the absence of repatriation (reimbursement) by the foreign company to the Swiss entity at the level of the confirmed adjustment amount.
